The Friendship
Decline

Why staying connected matters more than ever

scroll to explore ↓

Friendship is one of the most essential human experiences. It shapes our sense of belonging, supports our emotional health, and brings meaning to our daily lives.

Yet in today's world — where schedules are packed, communication is often digital, and responsibilities pile up — the time we spend with friends is quietly shrinking. The data tells a clear story: genuine in-person contact is one of the strongest predictors of happiness, emotional resilience, and even longevity.

Circles of Connection

Anthropologist Robin Dunbar discovered our relationships naturally fall into distinct layers — each one shaped by the limits of our emotional capacity, time, and attention.

Your Inner Circle

Intimates (3–5 people) are your innermost ring — usually a romantic partner or closest family. These are the people you'd turn to in a crisis.

Best Friends

Best Friends (~15 people) form your core support group. 5 out of 15 of your best friends make part of your core support group.

Friends & Active Network

Friends (~50) and your full active network (~150) are people you maintain genuine relationships with — though with less intensity.

The Outer Edge

Humans can cognitively manage up to 500 acquaintances — faces and names we recognize but rarely invest in deeply. Only 38% of people meet Dunbar's standard of 5 close friends.

Even as we desire large social networks, the deepest connections we maintain remain few — and incredibly precious.

But here's what the data also reveals: as we get older, even those precious few connections get harder to maintain.

How We Spend Our Time

Looking at daily hours spent with different people from ages 18 to 34, a clear pattern emerges. At first, friends dominate our social lives.

The Friend Drop

Time spent with friends peaks in our early 20s — then falls sharply as we enter the workforce and take on new responsibilities.

Partners & Family Rise

Time with a partner, children, and coworkers steadily increases — replacing the space once held by friendships.

Alone Time Rises Too

Time spent alone quietly grows throughout our 20s and into our 30s — a signal of growing social isolation even as life appears fuller.

This decline isn't just about busier calendars — it reflects a broader social shift.

As our communication becomes more digital and less face-to-face, physical interactions with friends start to feel optional rather than essential. But how many close friends do Americans actually report having?

Counting Close Friends

When Americans are asked how many close friends they have, the results are striking. Let's look at each group.

The Lonely 15%

8% of Americans have zero close friends. Another 7% have just one. Nearly 1 in 6 Americans navigate life with almost no close social bonds.

The Middle Ground

14% have two close friends and 18% have three — the largest single group. For many, a handful of trusted people is the reality of adult friendship.

Meeting Dunbar's Standard

Only 38% of Americans have 5 or more close friends — meeting Dunbar's threshold for a healthy core support group. The majority fall short.

Despite knowing this, most Americans still say friendship is one of the most important things in their lives.

The gap between how much we value friendship and how much time we actually invest in it reveals a tension at the heart of modern social life.

Friendship Is Highly Valued

Despite shrinking time together, Americans overwhelmingly say friendship is central to their emotional lives. Let's see how they rate its importance.

Very Important

The largest group — around half of Americans — say friendship is very important to them. It sits at the core of their sense of identity and belonging.

Rather Important

Another large share say friendship is rather important. Together, these two groups make up the vast majority of Americans who deeply value their social bonds.

A Small Minority Disagree

Only a small fraction say friendship is not very or not at all important. The data is clear: Americans cherish friendship — even when life makes it hard to nurture.

So if we value friendship so much — why do so many of us struggle to maintain it?

Research points to three consistent barriers that prevent Americans from forming and keeping close friendships.

Friendship & Happiness, Globally

Across many countries, researchers tracked happiness levels for people who often spend time with friends versus those who rarely do. The pattern is remarkably consistent.

The Happiness Gap

In almost every country studied, people who often spend time with friends report higher happiness than those who rarely do. The gap can be as large as 15 percentage points — as seen in Spain.

The Outliers

Moldova has the lowest happiness at just 39%. Iran is the country with the least difference between groups — 0%. Meanwhile, Canada & Singapore top the chart at 96% happiness among those who socialize often.

Where Does the US Stand?

The United States sits in the upper range — but still shows a measurable gap between frequent and infrequent socializers, reinforcing that spending time with friends is a genuine happiness driver.

Connection Is a Choice

Friendships are among the strongest predictors of happiness, yet many of us spend less time nurturing them as adult responsibilities grow.

Social scientists have found that frequent, positive interactions with friends stimulate the release of dopamine and oxytocin — the brain's "feel-good" chemicals. Over time, this contributes to greater psychological health and even improved physical health outcomes.

The data is clear: people who make space for meaningful social time are consistently happier. Reclaiming even small moments of shared time can strengthen these bonds — and remind us how essential real connection is to our well-being.
